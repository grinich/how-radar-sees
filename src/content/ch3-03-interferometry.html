Two eyes see motion

Split the antenna into two phase centres a short distance apart and you get two nearly-identical images. Multiply one by the complex conjugate of the other and the stationary world vanishes: motionless clutter produces no phase difference between the two looks, but a moving target does, and that phase difference is a direct measure of its radial velocity. This is along-track interferometry.

A close cousin, displaced phase-center antenna (DPCA), spaces the two looks by exactly the distance the satellite travels in one pulse. Then the second antenna sees precisely the clutter the first saw a pulse earlier, and subtracting the two cancels the stationary world outright, leaving only movers. The catch is Earth's rotation: it smears the clutter into a broad ridge that DPCA alone cannot fully cancel across a wide swath.
