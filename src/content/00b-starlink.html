Meet the constellation

Starlink is a low-Earth-orbit mega-constellation: thousands of small satellites circling about 550 km up — low enough that each races over the horizon in a few minutes, so it takes a whole swarm to keep any one spot on Earth covered. Each spacecraft is flat-packed to stack dozens per rocket, unfolds a single large solar array, and points a phased-array antenna down at the ground. They relay your data to pizza-box terminals below and to each other through laser links, handing you off from one satellite to the next as they streak past at 7.6 km/s.

The numbers are what make it unprecedented. Starlink went from a few dozen satellites in 2019 to roughly eight thousand working today — by far the largest constellation ever flown, and still climbing by the thousands each year. SpaceX is licensed for about twelve thousand and has filed for as many as forty-two thousand. Drag the year to watch it grow.

And the fleet is not one thing. It has moved through generations — the early v1.5, today's workhorse V2 Mini, and the giant new V3 that rides Starship to orbit and can talk straight to an ordinary phone. Each generation is larger, with a bigger antenna and more power. Drag the model to look around it, and switch generations to see them grow.

Every one of these was built to sell internet. But a large, powerful, electronically-steered antenna pointed at the ground is also the heart of a radar. The rest of this essay works out, from first principles, whether that resemblance is a coincidence — beginning with a single echo.
