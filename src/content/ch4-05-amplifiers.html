From bits to radiated power: the RF chain

Behind every antenna element is a radio-frequency chain. Transmitting, it turns digital samples into an analog wave, up-converts to the carrier frequency, and amplifies; receiving, it runs the same steps in reverse, with a low-noise amplifier coddling an impossibly faint signal before an analog-to-digital converter hands it to the processor. Modern arrays push more of this into software-defined radio on a chip — flexible, but power-hungry.

The amplifier's compromise

The power amplifier forces the compromise that decides what Starlink could do as a radar. A transistor is a valve, faithful only in its middle range: drive it too hard and the peaks clip; let it idle in the linear zone and it wastes power as heat.

How you bias that valve sets the amplifier's class — the trade of linearity for efficiency.

What decides how hard you must back off is the peak-to-average power ratio. A clean radar chirp barely varies, so the amplifier runs flat-out and efficient; Starlink's dense, many-beam OFDM signals have violent peaks, forcing wasteful headroom — but a radar-optimised waveform run in short bursts could briefly pour several times the normal power into a beam.
