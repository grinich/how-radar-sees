Hearing a whisper next to a scream

Here is radar's cruelest problem. The pulse you transmit is overwhelmingly louder than the echo you hope to hear — for a space radar listening for a faint return, the transmitter can be nearly two hundred decibels stronger than the signal. That is a factor of a hundred billion billion. Detecting the echo while shouting is like hearing a pin drop during a rocket launch. The art of managing this is duplexing, and it comes down to stacking up isolation from every trick available.

One row on that ladder changes everything: spatial separation. If the transmitter and receiver are on different satellites, the sheer distance between them supplies most of the isolation for free. This "bistatic" arrangement — one Starlink illuminating, another listening — sidesteps the whole problem, and it is the key that unlocks the most powerful modes.

Why care so much? Because if you can listen and transmit at the same time — full duplex — you escape the tyranny of pulses. A normal radar must go quiet to listen, capping how long and how often it can shout. Break that limit and you can run a continuous wave, pour in far more energy, sample motion far faster, and see a much wider swath at once.
