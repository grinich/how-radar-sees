Steering a beam with phase

Every Starlink satellite steers its beams electronically with a phased array: fire every element together and the waves add up straight ahead; delay each element a little more than its neighbour and the wavefront tilts, aiming the beam without moving a thing. Drag the steering and watch the pattern respond — and note how energy leaks into grating lobes when the elements are spaced wrong or the array is driven off its design frequency.

That flat polar plot is really a slice through a three-dimensional shape. The beam is a lobe — a narrow finger of energy pointing where you steer it, wrapped in smaller side lobes. Drag it around.

Beam squint: the wideband curse

Here is the catch that shapes everything. A phase shifter that steers one frequency correctly steers a different frequency to a slightly different angle. A radar-grade wideband signal contains a whole span of frequencies, so an analog phased array smears them across a range of directions — beam squint. The bigger and higher-gain the array, the narrower its beam and the more damage squint does. Widen the bandwidth and steer off-boresight to watch the beam fall apart by colour.

The fix is a hybrid array: group elements into tiles, give each tile a true time delay (which is frequency-flat), and let analog phase shifters do the fine steering within a tile. Fewer elements per tile means less squint — at the cost of gain and complexity. Slide the tile size to trade one against the other.

A quarter-turn of polarization

One more knob per element: the direction the electric field oscillates, its polarization. Feed an element from two perpendicular directions with a phase shift between them and the field rotates — circular polarization — which talks cleanly to tumbling phones and, as Part 5 will use, hands a radar a free ~30 dB of isolation because a circular wave flips its handedness when it reflects.
