The super-weapon: Direct-to-Cell

The V3 direct-to-cell antenna exists to connect ordinary phones to satellites, so it is enormous — about fifteen metres across — and it works at long S-band wavelengths that sail through weather and even through the plastic shells of drones. Remember that a big antenna helps as the square of its area. This one is sixteen times the area of its predecessor. The result is almost absurd: it reaches a usable image while radiating just a handful of watts.

At full tilt the numbers turn science-fictional. With its thousands of beams the antenna could, in bursts, image the entire planet at ten-metre resolution in near real time — the data and compute, not the physics, are the limit. More practically, using the spotlight video-SAR tracking of Part 3 it can lock onto a single moving target and film it: a fighter jet, a cruise missile, even a ballistic warhead on its arc through space. Small drones hide their plastic bodies but not their metal hearts; a stealth aircraft flying low can't hide its shadow on the ground below it. And every space-time-adaptive and multi-beam trick we built earlier applies — this one antenna could run them all.

This is one antenna, on a satellite designed to sell phone service. There are hundreds on orbit already, and thousands planned. All that remains is to count the cost — of the data, and of the capability itself.
